Method

How every verdict is built

Three questions, always in the same order. Diet first, because that is why you are here. Then what could make you ill. Then, for packaged food, how good it is for you.

  1. 01

    Vegan, vegetarian, lacto-vegetarian or non-veg?

    Every ingredient is traced to its source: plant, mineral, microbe, milk, egg, insect or slaughter. Each one says whether an animal was killed. When the source is unclear, the result says so, and Strict mode treats it as avoid.

    • 332curated entries, each with its source and process
    • 179with a vegetarian or vegan alternative
  2. 02

    Gluten and the 14 major allergens

    Labels are read the way the law defines them: EU Annex II, which also covers the US top nine. The legal exemptions are respected. Set your allergens once, and every result warns you, including “may contain” traces.

    • 191index entries with no gluten source
    • 14allergens checked on every label
  3. 03

    The Ledger health rating

    For packaged food: nutrition 50%, processing 25%, additives 25%. Each part is weighted by the strength of peer-reviewed evidence. Additive warnings come only from regulators and the WHO’s cancer agency, and every claim links to its source.

    • 3parts, each shown on its own bar
    • 0points for the organic label, which has no proven health advantage

Field guide

How to read the answers — and verify them.

What each verdict means, how to read a pack in under a minute, what to ask in a restaurant, and where the data comes from.

The five verdicts

Why milk is lacto-vegetarian but cheese isn't automatically. Milk (and ghee, butter, curd, paneer) is taken from a living animal — nothing is killed — so it counts as lacto-vegetarian. Cheese needs rennet, traditionally the stomach enzyme of slaughtered calves, so every cheese is treated as non-vegetarian — Parmesan, mozzarella, cheddar, feta, processed slices, cheese powder, all of it — unless the label itself says microbial, vegetable or "suitable for vegetarians" rennet (or carries the green-dot mark). The Scan tab applies the same rule: cheese in a list is flagged non-veg unless that wording appears in the text. Honey and beeswax are treated as vegetarian but not vegan.
Strict mode (the "Strict" switch at the top, on by default) changes only ingredients with an unclear source — "natural flavouring", E471, "emulsifier", unnamed glycerine — from Check to Avoid until the maker confirms the source. “May contain” and other shared-line warnings are not recipe ingredients and are shown separately; Strict does not assess cross-contact risk.
Was an animal killed? Every entry now answers this. Where no animal is killed — milk, honey, wool wax, horse-serum antivenoms, vaccines made in long-established cultured cell lines — it is listed as suitable for vegetarians, with a disclaimer where the product still has an animal origin. Eggs are the one exception by this site's rule: no animal is killed to collect them, but they count as non-vegetarian (change "I eat" to "Lacto-ovo vegetarian" if egg is fine for you). Vaccines grown in fertilised chick embryos stay non-vegetarian because the embryos die.
What "non-vegetarian" means here. Anything containing meat, fish or egg — or any essence, extract, stock, fat or derivative of them — plus ingredients made by killing animals or insects (gelatine, carmine, shellac, isinglass, animal rennet, bone char). The egg rule can be relaxed with the "Lacto-ovo vegetarian" setting at the top.

Reading a packet

The four-step read

  1. Look for the mark first. In India a green dot in a green square means vegetarian (no egg, meat or fish); a brown dot means non-veg. It says nothing about dairy or honey.
  2. Scan for the usual offenders. Gelatine, rennet, shellac, carmine, L-cysteine, "natural flavouring".
  3. Read the E-numbers, not the words. "Emulsifier (E471)" hides a fat source; "colour (E120)" is an insect.
  4. Read "may contain" separately. It is about shared equipment, not the recipe.

Words that mean animal

  • Gelatine — pig, cattle or fish collagen
  • Rennet — animal unless it says microbial/vegetable
  • Shellac, confectioner's glaze — insect resin
  • Carmine, cochineal, CI 75470 — crushed insects
  • Isinglass, fish sauce, bonito, dashi — fish
  • Albumen, lysozyme, ovalbumin — egg
  • Lard, tallow, suet, bone char — meat or bone
  • Whey, casein, lactose — dairy (lacto-ok, not vegan)

At a restaurant — six questions that get a real answer

"Is the stock vegetable, chicken or fish?"The single biggest hidden non-veg ingredient in soups, risotto, rice, gravies and curries.
"Is there fish sauce, oyster sauce or shrimp paste in the sauce or paste?"Thai, Vietnamese, Chinese, Korean and Indonesian kitchens assume yes unless told otherwise.
"Does the bread or batter contain egg?"Covers naan, buns, tempura, onion rings, noodles and pastry.
"Is the fryer used only for vegetarian items?"Answers the shared-oil question no menu prints.
"Is the cheese made with animal rennet?"Parmesan, Grana Padano, Pecorino, Gruyère are. Mozzarella, cheddar and feta vary by brand.
"Is there ghee or butter in the finish?"Vegetarian yes, vegan no — often added at the end, off-menu.

Medicines & vaccines

Know it, but don't skip care. Excipients are real: porcine gelatine in capsules, egg protein in flu vaccines, gelatine in some MMR and nasal-flu vaccines. Often there is an alternative: Flublok/Supemtek (recombinant) or Flucelvax (cell-grown) are egg-free flu vaccines; mRNA vaccines (Comirnaty, Spikevax, mRNA flu) contain no egg, gelatine, meat or fish; Shingrix replaced gelatine-containing Zostavax; Priorix replaces gelatine-containing MMR VaxPro (though still grown in chick-embryo cells). For medicines: HPMC capsules, levothyroxine instead of pig thyroid, estradiol instead of Premarin, algal omega-3 instead of fish oil, lichen D3 or D2 instead of lanolin D3. Never stop a prescribed medicine or skip a vaccine on this alone — ask your pharmacist or doctor for an alternative, and for religious questions ask your own authority.

Where the answers come from

The built-in index is a curated reference of 332 ingredients, additives, cheeses, dishes, drinks, medicines, vaccines and "hidden" label terms. It works offline and drives every verdict on the Scan tab and every live product check.

Live lookups go straight from your browser to these public services. There is no account and no server of ours. Your profile and recently checked products are kept only in this browser:

  • Open Food Facts / Open Beauty Facts — crowd-sourced packaged-product ingredient lists and barcodes (food, drink, cosmetics)
  • openFDA drug labels & NIH DailyMed — official US medicine and vaccine labelling, including inactive-ingredient (excipient) lists
  • Wikipedia — background on what something is and how it's made
  • OpenStreetMap (Nominatim geocoder, Overpass) — restaurants, with diet tags; Open-Meteo geocoding as backup; map tiles by OpenStreetMap
  • TheMealDB & TheCocktailDB — free recipe and cocktail databases; every ingredient is checked (egg white, honey, cream, fish sauce…)
  • USDA FoodData Central — official US branded-food database with full ingredient lists (shared demo key, or your own free key)
  • Spoonacular (optional, your own key) — packaged products and barcodes with diet badges, recipes filtered to your diet, and chain-restaurant menu items
  • NIH RxNorm — corrects misspelled medicine names
  • Open Food Facts ingredient taxonomy — second opinion on ingredients the index doesn't know (Scan tab)
  • Open Products Facts — barcode fallback for non-food consumables
  • Open Prices — where a product was recently on a shop shelf, and what shoppers have recently seen in a given store
  • Fruityvice — whole-fruit facts
  • Camera & photo tools — barcode reading uses your browser's built-in detector (or ZXing), label reading uses Tesseract.js; images never leave your device
  • r.jina.ai reader — only if you use "Read page"; it receives the address you enter

Known limits

  • Formulations change. A brand can switch rennet supplier or capsule shell without changing the pack front. Treat every verdict as a starting question.
  • Scans only know what's in the index. A clean scan means nothing flagged — "natural flavouring", "emulsifier" and "spices" can still hide animal sources; those are flagged as Check.
  • Crowd-sourced data can be wrong or stale, and its own vegan/vegetarian tags are algorithmic, not certification. Where it disagrees with the index, trust the stricter one and read the pack.
  • Restaurant data is volunteer-maintained. Confirm with the restaurant — especially shared fryers, stock and egg.
  • Not medical, religious or allergy advice. "Safe" means "consistent with the diet", not allergen-free or halal.
